How to hedge GPU rental costs

If you plan to rent GPUs next month or next quarter, you are exposed to whatever the hourly rate is when you start. Here are the three ways teams deal with that, and when each one makes sense.

Why GPU budgets are hard to plan

GPU rental is priced per GPU-hour, and that price moves. New chip generations, large model launches, new data centres coming online and demand in a particular region all push hourly rates up or down, sometimes within weeks. For a training run that needs thousands of GPU-hours, a move of a dollar an hour changes the bill by thousands of dollars.

Most teams find out what their run costs only when they start it. Hedging means deciding in advance how much of that uncertainty you are willing to carry.

The three options

1. Pay on-demand
Rent at whatever the price is when you need the GPUs. No commitment and nothing upfront, but you take the full price risk, and capacity may be short when demand spikes.
2. Reserve capacity
Sign a committed contract with a provider, usually for months or years. You get a lower, fixed rate and guaranteed GPUs, but you pay whether or not you use them, and you can't switch if prices fall.
3. Buy a price cap
Pay a small fee upfront for a ceiling on the price over a future window. If prices rise, you pay no more than the cap. If they fall, you pay the lower market price or rent elsewhere, and the fee is your only cost.

A cap works like insurance on your hourly rate, though it is a pricing agreement and not an insurance policy. It is the only one of the three that protects you from rises while letting you benefit from falls.

A worked example

Say you need 100 H100 GPU-hours next month and today's rate is about $3 an hour. You pay $20 upfront for a $3.00 cap on that window.

At a larger scale: 64 H100s for one week is 10,752 GPU-hours. A $2.50 cap for a $0.25 per-hour fee costs $2,688 upfront. If that week averages $3.80, you pay $2.75 an hour all-in instead of $3.80, about $11,290 less than renting at the market price.

Figures are examples to show how the arithmetic works, not quotes. Real fees depend on the GPU, the window and how much prices have been moving.

How to choose

  • Steady, long-running workloads (production inference that runs all year): reserved capacity usually gives the lowest rate.
  • A planned run, especially if the timing or size might change or prices could go either way: a cap sets the most you will pay and keeps your options open.
  • Small, flexible, or experimental work: on-demand is often fine, because the price risk is small in dollar terms.

Many teams combine them: reserve the base load, cap the spikes they can see coming, and pay on-demand for the rest.

Questions

What is a GPU-hour?

One GPU running for one hour. Eight GPUs for ten hours is 80 GPU-hours.

What price does a cap settle against?

Dodona caps settle on the average of a public daily GPU rental index over your window, so one unusual day can't trigger or dodge the payout. See the methodology.

Do I get the GPUs, or only the price?

Each Dodona agreement is tied to capacity from a GPU provider, so you can claim the compute at your price before the deadline. You can also leave it unused, or list it for another buyer.

What if I don't need it any more?

An unused cap expires and the upfront fee is not refunded. Before the window starts you can try to sell it to another user.
