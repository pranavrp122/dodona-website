The product What we sell

A cap protects the buyer from spikes. A floor protects the neocloud from slumps.

A cap or floor settles once, on the average over its window, so no single day decides it. Real GPU-hours change hands.

Price data from Ornn's index, for demonstration only. The cap and floor levels are our own illustration.

One picture for the whole product. A cap gives a buyer the right to rent a reserved block in a set window at no more than the cap level, for a cap fee paid up front. A floor gives a neocloud the right to rent its hours to a floor buyer at no less than the floor level. Both are options, not obligations, and both settle on the average over the window, so one spiky day can't trigger them or dodge them.

Both end in delivery. If a buyer doesn't need the hours, it resells them to another business. A floor buyer is a large GPU user that wants cheap capacity in a weak market. A cap and a floor together are called a collar. Slide 9 covers floors for lenders.

The chart. Each contract settles once, on the average over its window. August's daily prices averaged $2.77 and September's $2.83, our computation from Ornn's daily settles. An illustrative $2.85 floor on August covers the 8 cent gap below it, and an illustrative $2.75 cap on September covers the 8 cent gap above it. The daily spikes in grey don't trigger anything on their own. The levels are chosen to show both cases on real data. In the demo and on slide 7 the example cap is $2.50 for a week about a month out.

One sequence is still open. The window's average is known only after the hours have elapsed, so the term sheet must fix when the buyer exercises, when the hours are delivered and when the invoice is sent. Appendix B sets out that open decision.

Why options. Operators need the GPUs, not a cash difference, and a CFO can budget one fee but not margin calls.

They work together. Ornn, CME and ICE are building the benchmark. When futures list, neoclouds and buyers can hedge their contracts on them, and the price they settle on is the one we use.

Sources. CFTC filing for CME; ICE notice, 29 Sep 2026; 17 CFR 32.3. Counsel has to confirm the exemption before launch.

Why options are what operators need. A buyer wants a ceiling with an exit, the way they buy insurance. A future removes the upside and adds margin calls. Non-financial companies don't run margin accounts, and a CFO can budget a fee. Capacity is the real risk, and a future can't deliver GPUs, while our cap is tied to a reservation. The supply side has idle hours and expensive debt. Selling caps earns money on the hours, and floors protect the debt.

Complementary, not rivals. Futures give the index price discovery. Options are what operators buy. When CME or ICE list, neoclouds and buyers can hedge their contracts on them. Dodona itself never takes a side. Ornn is building the benchmark, we build the product that uses it, and we need their data licence to do it.

Our own worked example. The $0.25 cap fee assumes a $2.70 forward price for that week, a month ahead, at 40% volatility. The buyer pays the cap fee and the capped rent, nothing else. Sources for the reasons are in the notes.

Tell it as a story. A startup needs 64 H100s for a week in November, which is 10,752 GPU-hours. It caps them at $2.50 for a $0.25 cap fee, so $2,688 up front.

If the week averages $3.80, it pays $2,688 plus $2.50 × 10,752 = $26,880, so $29,568 in total, or $2.75 an hour. At the market it would pay $3.80 × 10,752 = $40,857.60, so it saves $11,290. If the week averages $2.30, it walks away from the cap, rents at $2.30 for $24,729.60 and pays $27,417.60 in total, or $2.55 an hour. A fixed $2.80 would have cost $30,105.60, so it pays $2,688 less. If plans change, it resells the hours. With a resale bid of $3.70 in the spike case, it gets 10,752 × $1.20 = $12,902 more than the capped price, or $10,214 after the cap fee. In the pilot the first resale bidders are the partner neocloud's own spot customers.

A caveat to say before they do. We assume a forward price of $2.70 for a week about a month out, and at 40% volatility our model values the $2.50 cap at $0.25. With an assumed forward of $2.80 for that week, the cap would be worth about $0.33. If the delivery-window forward were $3.02, a $2.50 cap would have a lower bound of about $0.52 before discounting. Our $0.25 example instead assumes a $2.70 forward for that week. Today's Ornn spot of $3.02 alone does not set that bound, and the assumed forward is neither a forecast nor a market quote. The example teaches the shape, not a live quote.

Tell it as a story. Northwind, an example neocloud, has 1,000 H100s, and expects about 300 of them to go unsold next month. The cap needs those GPUs free for the whole window. GPUs that sit idle only on nights and weekends would back shorter windows instead, such as a weekend cap. That's 300 × 730 = 219,000 GPU-hours a month. It sells a one-month cap on those hours at $3.02, today's Ornn level, for a window starting next month. The cap fee is about 0.4 × volatility × square root of time × price, with time = 4/36 of a year, so the square root is 0.333. At 26.2% volatility, Silicon Data's H100 history and the low-swing case, that's 0.4 × 0.262 × 0.333 × 3.02 = $0.1055 a GPU-hour, so 219,000 × $0.1055 = $23,105. Our 15% cut is $3,466, so Northwind keeps $19,639. At 40%, our base case, the cap fee is $0.1611 and the month earns $35,281. Both cap fees use the rule of thumb, about 0.4 × volatility × √time × price, rounded to $0.1055 and $0.1611. A moment-matched average-price calculation gives about $23,037 to $35,159, a gap of about 0.3% that doesn't change the example.

Gross versus net. At 26.2% the gross cap fee is $23,104.50, our 15% is $3,465.68 and Northwind keeps $19,638.83. At 40% the gross is $35,280.90, our 15% is $5,292.14 and Northwind keeps $29,988.77. If half the capped hours are delivered, our 1% takes another $3,306.90, which leaves Northwind $16,331.93 and $26,681.87 of cap fee after both fees. That is not Northwind's total revenue, since it is also paid for the compute it delivers.

What Northwind takes on. It earns an upfront cap fee and accepts a ceiling on the selling price of the reserved block. Its benefit depends on the fee, its delivery costs and other demand for those hours. If prices spike, another customer might have paid more than $3.02. If the buyer walks away late, there may be little time to re-let the hours. Northwind holds the capacity, and the 85% of the cap fee it keeps pays it for that commitment. Our 15% pays for our own services, which are origination, buyer sourcing, standard contracts, capacity verification, reporting and delivery coordination. When hours are delivered, our proposed 1% of the compute bill comes from Northwind's side, at most 219,000 × $3.02 × 1% = $6,614 a month at full delivery, or $3,306.90 if half are delivered.

Be honest about size. $23,105 to $35,281 a month is about 1% to 1.6% of that fleet's revenue at $3.02 an hour, 730,000 × $3.02 = $2,204,600 a month. The fee alone won't win a neocloud. The booked block and the lender story will. Demand for short deals is real. A floor gives a neocloud's lender a minimum revenue number to lend against.

$1.5M a month

in any month that averages below $2.00, because the floor buyer then takes every hour at $2.00.

Oil lenders already ask producers to lock in prices. We want GPU lenders to do the same with floors.

Our own worked example. Sources. AC Research, 16 Oct 2026; Lambda via Business Wire, 1 Oct 2026; DCD on Nebius, 22 Oct 2026; CCIR on CoreWeave; CoreWeave 8-Ks; Oil & Gas Journal.

How the floor works. It's physical, like the cap. The neocloud buys the right to rent its hours to a floor buyer at $2.00. When a month averages below $2.00, the neocloud delivers to the floor buyer at $2.00 instead of selling at the market. The floor buyer is a large GPU user that wants cheap capacity in a weak market. No cash payout replaces delivery, which matches slide 15.

The example. 1,024 H100s that aren't on long contracts make 747,520 GPU-hours a month. For six months the neocloud buys a $2.00 floor and pays for it by selling a $3.00 cap, a pair called a collar. Below $2.00 it earns 747,520 × $2.00 = $1,495,040 a month. In a $1.70 month that's $224,256 more than the market. In a $3.40 month the cap buyer takes the hours at $3.00, $299,008 less than the market. This exact pair isn't free. If the market expects $2.80 for six months at 40% volatility, selling the $3.00 cap pays for a floor near $2.64. The $2.00 and $3.00 pair costs about nothing only if the market expects around $2.45.

Loan maths, if asked. With running costs of $0.60 per GPU-hour, our assumption, a month below $2.00 leaves (2.00 − 0.60) × 747,520 = about $1.05M of income. At 1.35x coverage that supports about $775,000 a month of loan payments. A floor is only as good as its buyer, so a lender will check the floor buyer can take and pay for every hour.

Business model How we make money

We earn 15% of each cap fee and 1% of each compute bill delivered through us.

Proposed pilot pricing, taken from the neocloud's side of each trade. We never take the other side, and the money passes through an escrow agent.

Proposed fee model and our own illustration, not a forecast. Comparables and the arithmetic are in the notes.

Say the model in one breath. 15% of each cap fee, and 1% of each compute bill delivered through us, paid out of the neocloud's side. Nothing else per trade. We never take the other side and never hold funds. These are proposed pilot rates, not validated market pricing, and counsel reviews any per-trade fee before launch.

The ladder, our arithmetic. The cap fee is $0.1611 per GPU-hour at 40% volatility for a one-month window starting next month at $3.02. That is a rounded rule-of-thumb value. The moment-matched average-price value is about $0.1605, an immaterial difference. Our 15% is $0.02417 per capped GPU-hour. The main case assumes half of capped hours are delivered through us, used or resold, at $3.02, so our 1% adds $0.0151 per capped hour. Half delivered is a business assumption, not a measured delivery rate. Under the model, the risk-neutral chance that this at-the-money cap on a month starting next month finishes in the money is about 47.3% at 40% volatility, so 50% is a reasonable round illustration. Buyers who walk away rent elsewhere, and none of that bill passes through us. At 100,000 capped GPU-hours a month, that's $2,416.50 plus $1,510 = $3,926.50 a month, or $47,118 a year. At 1M, $24,165 plus $15,100 = $39,265 a month, or $471,180 a year. At 10M, $241,650 plus $151,000 = $392,650 a month, or $4,711,800 a year. If every capped hour is delivered, the 1% line doubles. That gives $5,436.50, $54,365 and $543,650 a month, or $65,238, $652,380 and $6,523,800 a year. At 26.2% volatility the cap-fee line falls by about a third. 1M GPU-hours a month is about 1,370 GPUs running full time. Synergy's $25B of neocloud revenue at a blended $3 is about 694M GPU-hours a month, so 10M is about 1.4%. These are illustrative annual revenue figures at the stated fee, volume and delivery assumptions, before operating and transaction costs. They are not a forecast or contracted revenue.

Model value versus what the neocloud nets. If $0.1611 is the model value of the option, the neocloud nets 85% of it minus the 1% on delivered compute, about $0.1218 per capped hour at 50% delivery, 24.4% below the model value. That isn't a loss, since the neocloud is also paid for the compute it delivers. The pilot has two coherent choices. Either the buyer pays the model value and the neocloud accepts the lower net because Dodona brings it the buyer and the booking. Or the customer price is grossed up so the neocloud still nets the model value, about $0.207 at 50% delivery, $0.190 at none and $0.225 at full delivery, and buyers must accept that. The pilot tests which, and we haven't picked one.

The rules Regulation

Real GPUs change hands between businesses, and we never hold the money.

  • Real GPU-hours are delivered, never a cash payout instead.
  • Only businesses that use or sell GPUs take part.
  • We designed it for the CFTC's trade option exemption. Counsel has to confirm before launch.
  • We never take a side and never hold customer money.

Not legal advice. Sources. 17 CFR 32.3; 77 FR 48208; CFTC 7343-16; CFTC release 9286-26, 19 Aug 2026; docket CFTC-2026-1850.

Why we resell instead of paying out cash. Under the CFTC's Brent interpretation, swapping a delivery for a cash difference has to be a separate deal agreed later. It can't be written into the original contract. A built-in cash payout, or one most buyers use, would weaken the intent to deliver that the trade option exemption needs. Reselling delivered hours keeps delivery real, and floors work the same way, since the floor buyer takes the hours. 77 FR 48208 https://www.cftc.gov/LawRegulation/FederalRegister/FinalRules/2012-18003.html

17 CFR 32.3 https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/32.3 ; 2016 amendments removed Form TO for end users https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/7343-16 . The CFTC asked for public comment on compute derivatives in August 2026, and its chair said America can't win the AI race without a strong derivatives market for compute https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9286-26 ; docket https://www.regulations.gov/document/CFTC-2026-1850-0001 , comments closed 20 Oct 2026.

Counsel has to confirm three things before launch. The contracts fit the trade option exemption. Netting a resale through the escrow agent counts as resale. And the 1% compute fee and our cut of cap fees don't need a broker licence. Before the fee and venue questions can be settled, the term sheet must also fix the exercise, delivery and invoice sequence, which Appendix B marks as open. The open question is whether many buyers and sellers can trade before contracts expire, which is why we start with one-to-one quotes. Appendix A Questions we expect

Ten questions we expect.

Why caps and not futures?
Futures tie both sides to a price, and none trade yet. Buyers want a cap. Neoclouds want a floor and the cap fee income.
Who's on the other side of the contract?
For a cap, the partner neocloud that sells it on a block of GPUs it has set aside. For a floor, a large GPU user that must take the hours at the floor level. Dodona matches the two sides.
What if every buyer uses their cap at once?
Delivery can't go beyond the reserved block. Resale happens at a real bid, so nobody gets paid for hours that weren't actually sold.
What if the neocloud can't deliver?
It owes damages under its service agreement. It loses a deposit held by an escrow agent. And a clause in the contract makes it supply replacement capacity.
Do you need a licence?
Our analytics don't need one. We designed the contracts to fit 17 CFR 32.3, for deals between businesses that end in real delivery. Counsel has to confirm that, and the venue question, before launch.
Why would a lender trust Dodona Value?
Anyone can reproduce it. Every number shows the method version, the date and a checksum of the input data. We don't trade, so we have no position to protect.
Can a neocloud push the price around?
We settle on an average, limit how much any one neocloud counts, and real hours stand behind each price.
What if prices fall?
Cap buyers lose only the cap fee they paid. Neoclouds with floors deliver at the floor level. Lenders need an independent value most when prices are falling.
Isn't the revenue small?
At first. A million capped GPU-hours a month earns about $470,000 a year under our proposed fees and assumptions if half the hours are delivered, or $650,000 if all are. Floors for lenders and Dodona Value subscriptions add to that.
What's real in the demo?
Only the Ornn price index, which is labelled demonstration only. Contracts, the companies on each side and payments are all simulated.

Method, in words. You can't store a GPU-hour, so we value off a forward price for the delivery window, the way EEX does for electricity. No compute forward curve trades yet, so in the demo the forward is the spot index carried at half of the clamped historical drift. That is a model forward, not a market forward, and a live quote needs a market or contracted forward. The engine, method black76-asian-v0, runs a Black-76 call on that forward with the variance time set to the window start plus a third of the window's length, floored at one week, and discounts at 5% a year to the window end. Floors come from put-call parity. Volatility is the sample standard deviation of weekly log returns × √52, falling back to daily × √365 with fewer than 8 weekly points. The third-of-the-window rule is a small-volatility approximation for an arithmetic average with uniform sampling. Uncertainty before the window starts remains, so for a month-long window starting a month out the effective time is 1/12 + 1/36 = 1/9 of a year. It is not an identity for arbitrary forward curves, jumps, mean reversion or usage schedules. Turnbull-Wakeman and Curran are the intended methods for the exact arithmetic average, and neither is implemented yet. CME advisory 20-198 moved four oil average-price options to Curran effective 18 May 2020, which supports averaging-specific valuation, not vanilla Black-76.

The tables. They use zero discounting, so they sit a fraction above the engine, whose check value for a $2.80 forward and strike, 40% and one month is about $0.128 after discounting and $0.129 before. The first table is a one-month cap for a window starting now at a $2.80 assumed forward. The rule of thumb, 0.4 × volatility × √time × price, matches Black-76 on the last day's price, and settling on the month's average makes the cap much cheaper. The second shows an assumed forward below the strike, which traders call backwardation. Caps get cheaper and floors get more expensive. Fixing convention. A real quote needs the observation dates, weights, index identifier, payment date, rate and rounding. Thirty end-of-day fixes over a month give about $0.067 and $0.076 at 35% and 40%, against $0.065 and $0.074 for a continuous average.

Our Ornn figures are our own calculation on Ornn's public daily index from 18 Jul to 17 Oct 2026, 92 daily points per GPU. We take the sample standard deviation of log returns. Daily volatility is annualised with the square root of 365. Weekly volatility uses every seventh daily point back from the latest, 14 points, annualised with the square root of 52. That gives 88.8% daily and 37.7% weekly for H100 SXM, over a price range of $2.49 to $3.21, and 39.0% daily and 60.2% weekly for B200. So the figures reproduce within a few points, and the difference comes from the window and the weekly anchor. The data is Ornn's public index, used for demonstration only. The two sources differ in index construction and in sample period, so the gap between them doesn't isolate either effect. Always quote volatility with index, period, return type, annualisation factor and sampling convention.
